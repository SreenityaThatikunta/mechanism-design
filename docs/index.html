When is honesty the best policy?

Three auction mechanisms, simulated 200,000 times per bid and checked against closed-form theory. Two of them reward telling the truth. One punishes it. The difference comes down to a single design choice.

> the-question

Does the mechanism reward truth?

An auction is a machine for turning private information into a decision. Bidders know what something is worth to them; the auctioneer does not. A strategyproof mechanism is one where the best thing a bidder can do is simply say what it knows — no guessing about rivals, no strategy at all. Not every auction has this property.

Part 1 · Second-price
Yes — weakly dominant

Your bid decides whether you win, never what you pay. Bidding your true value is the only bid that is optimal against every rival bid.

Part 2 · First-price
No — truth earns zero

You pay your own bid, so bidding your value hands over your entire surplus. The optimum is to shade down to (n−1)/n of it.

Part 3 · VCG
Yes — dominant

Each agent is paid its marginal contribution, computed entirely from other agents' reports. Lying cannot move your own price.

> part-1

Second-price sealed-bid auction

Bidder 1 holds a fixed value v and chooses a bid b. Rivals draw values from U[0,1] and bid by one of three rules. The winner pays the highest rival bid, so bidder 1's profit is v − m.

Change the value, the rival strategy and the number of rivals below. The curve moves a great deal. The location of its peak never does.

simulated (200,000 auctions) closed form b = v

A rival bidding truthfully and a rival drawing a fresh U[0,1] number both produce bids uniform on [0,1] — from bidder 1's side they are the same thing, which is why those two settings give an identical curve. A rival that halves its value never bids above 0.5, so past b = 0.5 the curve flattens: bidder 1 already wins every auction.

Truthfulness, stated precisely

Against any single fixed rival bid, many bids tie for best — if the rival bids 0.1 and your value is 0.5, then 0.2, 0.5 and 1.0 all win and all pay 0.1. Truthfulness only becomes special once you ask which bid works against every rival bid at once.

> part-2

First-price auction

Same setup, one rule changed: the winner now pays its own bid. That single change destroys truthfulness. Rivals play the symmetric equilibrium b = ((n−1)/n)·v; bidder 1 deviates by bidding k·v.

simulated closed form equilibrium k = (n−1)/n truthful k = 1

Drag k to 1.0 — truthful bidding — and the expected utility reads exactly zero, for every n. The only auctions you win at k = 1 are the ones where you give away everything you were playing for.

The winner always overpays

In 100% of simulated auctions the winner could have bid less and still won. That is ~100% by construction — bids are continuous, so the winner almost surely strictly outbid the runner-up. The informative number is the size of the gap, and it is large: the winner pays a quarter to a half more than it needed to.

In a second-price auction this gap is zero by definition — the winner pays the runner-up's bid exactly. That is the entire design argument for charging the second price.

> part-3

VCG: buying the cheapest path

Now the auction is a procurement. Every edge of this network is an agent with a private cost, and we need to buy a path from A to F. VCG picks the cheapest path and pays each edge its marginal contribution: what the best path would have cost without it, minus the cost of the rest of the path chosen.

chosen path not used outlined = the misreporting edge

Everything above is recomputed live in your browser — Dijkstra plus the VCG payment rule, with every other edge reporting honestly. Drag the slider and watch what the liar gets.

With everyone honest the mechanism picks ABEF and pays AB 4, BE 2, EF 3, and every other edge 0 — reproducing Figure 7.3 of Shoham & Leyton-Brown exactly. Note that BE and EF both cost 1, yet EF is paid 3 and BE only 2. VCG prices market power, not cost: the network without EF (7) is worse than the network without BE (6).

Why lying never pays

Pick AB — an edge on the path — and sweep its report. Its payment stays pinned at 4 no matter what it says, because the payment formula contains only other agents' costs. Under-reporting changes nothing; over-reporting past ~4 just prices it off the path and drops its profit from 1 to 0.

Now pick AC, an edge off the path. It can buy its way on by reporting below 1 — and is then paid 1 against a true cost of 2, for a profit of −1. The only way to win is to win at a loss.

VCG is not free: it pays out 9 in total for a path that truly costs 5. That gap is the price of strategyproofness, and the reason VCG is not budget-balanced in procurement.

Simulation is not evidence on its own

A Monte Carlo that is only checked against another Monte Carlo can be confidently wrong. Every simulated quantity here has a closed form derived by hand, and the charts plot both together — the dashed line is theory, the dots are 200,000 simulated auctions each.
